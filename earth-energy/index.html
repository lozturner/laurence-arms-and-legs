05Where the market sits, May 2026

Researched, not vibed. Every number below has a citation in section 09. Three things have moved enough to actually change the plan.

What’s materially worse than 2021

The 2021 thesis leaned heavily on one number: ~79p per RTFC at a 1.634 credits/kg yield, which alone gave you ~£900k/yr per gasifier. Two things have moved against that.

  1. The price. The Platts/QCI RTFC assessment averaged 27.5p in December 2025, up from 25.4p in January 2025 [6]. At face value, that’s about a third of the 2021 number.
  2. The classification. After the post‑2022 RTFO reforms, tyre‑derived biomethane is treated as a Recycled Carbon Fuel, not a waste biofuel. RCFs receive single dRTFCs (1 per kg), not the doubled rate, and must clear a 65% GHG saving versus a 94 gCO2eq/MJ fossil baseline [7]. That breaks the “3.8 credits × 43% = 1.634” arithmetic in the 2021 doc.

Combined effect: the RTFC line in the original model is roughly a quarter to a third of what it used to be. The calculator below now defaults to 27.5p so you can see the impact yourself.

GGSS is the other dead end. It was extended to applications until 31 March 2028 and commissioning by 31 March 2030, with the 2026/27 budget held at £124.9m [8] — but it’s for AD biomethane only. A tyre gasifier doesn’t qualify. No subsidy route there.

What’s materially better than 2021

  1. The energy‑crisis backdrop didn’t end. Ofgem’s Q2 2026 default tariff cap is 24.67p/kWh electricity and 5.74p/kWh gas [1]. SME mid‑size fixed contracts are 20–25p/kWh electricity, micro‑businesses 26–30p, out‑of‑contract 32–45p, and SME gas 7.5–9p fixed / 11–14p deemed [2]. Cornwall Insight forecasts business bills staying ~70% above pre‑crisis levels [3]. The BCC reports 27% of firms struggling with energy costs and SME energy debt at ~£2bn [4]. UK Hospitality just (May 2026) issued a six‑point relief plan after 3,000+ hospitality closures in 2025 [5]. Selling behind‑the‑meter electricity at, say, 15p/kWh undercuts a typical industrial offtaker by 5–10p while clearing well above wholesale.
  2. Tyre‑disposal law just tightened. Defra confirmed in July 2025 it will abolish the T8 and T9 waste exemptions via the Waste Tyres Bill, now in the Commons. T8 compliance charges already hit £1,236 in the upper band, and the Environment Agency is mid‑way through a national inspection campaign (711 registered T8 sites as of Feb 2026) [10]. The Tyre Recovery Association is pushing for an EU/Australia‑style whole‑tyre export ban. The result: permitted homes for ~50m UK end‑of‑life tyres a year are scarcer, so gate‑fee economics should rise above the £40/t the 2021 doc used.
  3. Maritime decarbonisation is now a budgeted reality. Three concrete schemes you can point at: (a) Portsmouth Port’s Sea Change — a £19.8m grant, becoming the UK’s first multi‑berth shore‑power chargeport from spring 2025, targeting >20,000 tCO₂e/yr from 2027 [12]. (b) Solent Freeport customs/tax site status with forecast 7.2% maritime GVA growth and capital‑allowance benefits [13]. (c) UK ETS extension to maritime emissions from July 2026 (CO₂, methane, N₂O) [14], creating a real carbon price on bunker fuel and a margin for liquefied biomethane bunkering — LNG/LBM bunkering globally already operates from 222 ports with 62 bunker vessels [15].

The wedge in plain English

If I were rebuilding the deck today I would not lead with a 9‑acre flagship plant on a remote site, and I would not lead with RTFC arbitrage. I would lead with a port‑sited modular tyre gasifier — inside the Solent Freeport, or a North‑East freeport equivalent — selling behind‑the‑meter electricity to a single anchor offtaker (cold store, ferry shore‑power load, cruise‑berth chargeport). ELT gate fees become the second revenue leg. RTFC sales and LBM bunkering are upside, not the base case. The pitch is “waste compliance + energy‑cost hedge”, not “credit arbitrage.”

Comparators worth naming

  • Powerhouse Energy / Peel NRE — DMG distributed modular gasification, Protos plastics‑to‑hydrogen plant near Ellesmere Port (~25 tpd), plus a £20m West Dunbartonshire site and an option pipeline for up to 70 facilities [16].
  • Velocys — commissioned a Fischer–Tropsch system at SuMo’s Wednesbury facility in March 2026, advancing the ClearSkies waste‑to‑SAF route with DfT Advanced Fuels Fund backing [17].

Both are plastics‑to‑hydrogen or waste‑to‑SAF, not tyre‑to‑biomethane. Which means the Prometheus niche is, awkwardly, still uncrowded — but also, awkwardly, still unproven at bankable scale. That cuts both ways.
